# How to choose a good first task

Pick a task that is useful enough to matter and safe enough to repeat.

1. Write down three things you did recently that involved several files,
   repeated copying, or the same instructions more than once.
2. Remove anything that uses private, confidential, irreplaceable, or
   high-stakes information.
3. Prefer a task with a visible result, such as a new summary, checklist,
   schedule, folder of renamed copies, or draft plan.
4. Choose a task whose correct result you can judge. If you cannot recognize a
   good answer, it is not a good first exercise.
5. Make a practice folder and place **copies** of the needed files in it. Use
   fictional details if the originals contain personal information.
6. Define a small finish line. “Create a one-page brief and list unanswered
   questions” is better than “help with this project.”

A strong first task is reversible, takes about 20 to 45 minutes by hand, uses
two to five inputs, and ends in one or two outputs. It gives the agent enough
work to show its value without giving it a chance to cause serious harm.

Good examples include turning fictional meeting notes into an action list,
combining public articles into a source table, or making a study guide from
course notes you are allowed to use.

Save sending messages, changing real accounts, handling client or patient
information, spending money, publishing, and deleting files for later—after
you understand the product's controls and your organization's rules.

# How to choose a desktop AI agent

You do not need the best product. You need one you are allowed to use, can
give a bounded workspace, and can review. Most people can decide in about
fifteen minutes.

Work through these questions in order. Stop at the first one that decides the
answer for you.

## 1. Does someone else already decide this?

Check whether your workplace, school, client, or professional body has an
approved product, a managed account, or an AI-use policy. If it does, use that
route. An approved product you may actually use beats a better product you may
not.

Ask an administrator when the policy is unclear. Do not install a personal
account to work around a restriction.

## 2. Where does the work already live?

Match the form to the material, not to a review score. The forms and their
tradeoffs are compared in [Ways desktop agents come
packaged](../start-here/ways-desktop-agents-come-packaged.md).

| If your task material is… | Start with… |
| --- | --- |
| documents, spreadsheets, slides, research, or mixed files | a desktop app |
| files you already keep open in a code or text editor | an editor add-on |
| a folder you already work in by command, or repeated file operations | a command-line tool |
| work that should continue while you do something else | a browser or cloud worker, after you understand what gets uploaded |

If you are unsure, choose a desktop app. It shows the most about what the
agent is doing and asks for the least new skill.

## 3. Can it do the four things this method needs?

Before committing, confirm the product can do all four. Any product that
cannot is a poor fit for learning, whatever else it offers.

- **Limit the workspace.** You can point it at one folder or a named set of
  files rather than everything you own.
- **Ask before acting.** It requests approval for actions that change, send,
  publish, spend, or delete, and names the target.
- **Show what changed.** You can see which files it created, changed, moved,
  or deleted—ideally with a before-and-after view.
- **Work from your material.** It can read the files you give it and write a
  result back, not only answer in a conversation.

Read the product's current data, permission, and security pages for these
answers. The interface alone does not tell you where work runs or what is
retained.

## 4. What else constrains you?

Weigh these only after the four requirements pass:

- **Cost.** Know whether a free tier is enough for a first task and what
  triggers a charge.
- **Where work runs.** Local installation does not guarantee local
  processing, and a cloud product does not automatically reach your accounts.
- **Devices and accounts.** Confirm operating-system support, sign-in
  requirements, and whether a work account is required.
- **Accessibility.** Check screen-reader support, keyboard operation, and text
  scaling if you rely on them.
- **Language.** Confirm it works well in the language your sources use.

## 5. Try it on one bounded task

Judge a product by a real, small result rather than by a feature list. Give
your top candidate the practice task in [Get
started](../getting-started.md) and notice:

- how clearly it stated its plan before acting;
- whether its permission requests named the action and the target;
- whether you could see exactly what it changed; and
- how much correction the result needed.

A product that hides its actions is a poor fit even when its writing is good.

## When to change your mind

Switching later is normal and cheap at this stage. Your task briefs, checks,
and safety habits transfer between products; only the buttons change.

Reconsider when a product cannot narrow access to the material you work with,
when you cannot tell what it changed, when your organization's policy changes,
or when the work moves to a place the product cannot reach.
